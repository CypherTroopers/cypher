Executive Summary
Today’s AI competition is rapidly evolving into a capital-intensive infrastructure race. Models, data, and
algorithms remain essential, yet the bulk of the cost comes from chips, power, networks, and data
centers. Meta, for example, narrowed its full-year 2026 capital expenditure guidance to $130 billion-
$145 billion in the second quarter. Alphabet recorded $44.9 billion of capital expenditures in the second
quarter of 2026 alone, with the vast majority directed toward technical infrastructure supporting AI
investment. AI is moving from a traditional software business toward a capital-intensive infrastructure
business.

We particularly focus on two curves moving in opposite directions across the AI industry. As U.S.-China
AI competition continues to intensify, leading AI companies and cloud providers must keep securing
next-generation GPUs, accelerators, and data-center capacity to preserve advantages in model
performance and compute. Capital expenditure and depreciation pressure keep rising. At the same
time, supply models represented by Chinese open-weight models such as Qwen and low-cost APIs such
as DeepSeek are setting up new performance-to-price benchmarks and compressing the pricing room
for model-service tokens globally.

  The GPU arms race drives costs higher, while the token price war pushes unit revenue lower.
  CapEx ↑ + Token Price ↓ puts AI infrastructure returns under pressure from both sides.

Under this pressure, a second revenue curve is evolving from a useful way to improve utilization into a
practical requirement for AI companies seeking to repair asset returns. Beyond model APIs,
subscriptions, and token revenue, AI companies urgently need additional monetization channels so the
same GPUs purchased with substantial capital can continue generating cash flow when idle,
underutilized, or retired from frontier clusters.

AI chips also present a distinctive contradiction: their physical life is long, while their period of
economic leadership is short. Meta even extended the estimated useful life of certain servers and
network equipment to 5.5 years beginning in 2025. Yet in NVIDIA's published MLPerf Inference v5.0
results, an 8-GPU B200 system delivered roughly 3.1 times the throughput of an 8-GPU H200 system on
Llama 2 70B Interactive. Older GPUs have not stopped working; they may simply cease to be the best
assets for the most expensive, frontier AI workloads.

We call the resulting problem the economic depreciation gap: large volumes of chips remain fully
capable of computation, yet new generations of AI hardware cause them to be downgraded, left idle, or
removed from core clusters earlier than their physical life would imply.

For us, the strategic value of CPH is to create a second revenue curve for this compute capacity.

1. The Core Problem in AI Infrastructure: Chips Become Economically
Obsolete Too Quickly
For traditional industrial equipment, physical life and economic life are usually relatively close. AI chips
behave differently.

NVIDIA's official benchmarks show that Blackwell already delivers several-fold gains in per-GPU
performance over Hopper in certain LLM inference scenarios. In MLPerf Inference v5.0, for example,
B200 delivered about 3.1 times the H200 throughput on Llama 2 70B Interactive, while GB200 NVL72
delivered up to roughly 3.4 times the per-GPU performance of H200 on Llama 3.1 405B. At the same
time, NVIDIA explicitly noted that Hopper, three years after launch, could still run the latest workloads
and continue improving through software optimization.

Together, these two sets of data reveal the AI infrastructure financial problem that matters most to us:

Older chips still have compute capacity; compared to newer chips, however, the AI revenue they can
generate per watt, per dollar, and per rack declines.

AI companies therefore face a growing pool of GPUs moving from primary production assets into
secondary production assets. They can still compute, but without new uses, they are limited to
discounted rentals, resale, idling, or depreciation charges.

CPH creates a new option: keep those GPUs producing revenue through digital assets and compute
services.

When we refer to "falling token prices," we mean the broader direction of market pricing; we do not

capability improves and open-source alternatives multiply, the pricing power that can be sustained
per unit of model capability is coming under pressure. For AI companies, the economic implication is
straightforward: recovering GPU investment by raising token prices alone is becoming increasingly
difficult.

In our infrastructure recovery model, we can simplify the relationship as follows:

   Cash flow available to recover infrastructure investment = Token Volume Q × Token Price P +
   Second Monetization Revenue R₂ − GPU / Data-Center Cost C

If cost C rises while token price P falls, token volume Q must grow faster to recover the same
infrastructure investment. Put simply, the GPU arms race raises costs while the token price war lowers
unit revenue. If volume growth fails to keep pace with these two forces, AI companies face longer asset
payback periods, greater depreciation pressure, and tighter free cash flow.

CPH can directly address this gap by adding a monetization channel whose economics do not move in
perfect sync with model token pricing. When a GPU has no high-value AI workload, it can continue
generating value through CPH PoW or, in the future, through our compute marketplace. The additional
revenue R₂ can therefore reduce the share of the investment burden carried by the token business.

2. Our Core Business Logic: Give Every GPU Two Revenue Curves
The capital cost of an AI GPU is incurred when it is purchased. Once the purchase is complete, the
question that matters most to us is:

  How much total cash flow can this GPU generate before it becomes economically obsolete?

A traditional AI data center has one primary revenue curve: training and inference. With no customer
workload, GPU revenue approaches zero. When a device is no longer well suited to the most advanced
models, its economic value can fall rapidly.

With CPH, we want to add a backup revenue curve that does not depend on AI customers placing orders
in real time.

We view this model as analogous to peak-load management in the energy industry: AI is the high-value
primary load, while CPH is a flexible backup load that can be started or stopped as needed. The GPU

additional hardware purchases solely for mining.

For that reason, we care more about whether CPH can turn a GPU utilization problem into a yield
problem than about maximizing mining profit at any single point in time.


3. Why CPH Is Well Suited to the Second Revenue Curve
To make this logic work, we must establish a direct link between CPH and AI hardware and computing
resource demand. The traditional model—where “any computing power can be used for mining”—is far
from sufficient.

CPH uses the proprietary Colossus-X mining algorithm, which is specifically designed for GPU hardware.
Its mining process primarily utilizes GPU memory (VRAM) rather than relying on specialized ASIC mining

machines. As a result, the same GPUs that AI companies use for training and inference can participate in
the CPH network during idle periods without requiring any hardware modifications, allowing them to
generate additional revenue.

In our published white-paper, Cypherium: Road to AGI, we go further and propose using this GPU
network for AI training and inference. Nodes earn CPH by contributing compute; external enterprises
and users can eventually pay CPH to rent AI compute, while the marketplace matches demand with idle
computing resources around the world.

Under this design, CPH can perform three functions at the same time:

   1. Network security incentive: GPUs participate in PoW and earn CPH;
   2. Idle compute monetization: AI companies move unused GPUs into the network during low-load
      periods;
   3. AI compute settlement: Users who need training, inference, or other computing services buy and
      pay CPH, and compute providers receive CPH.
For us, the third layer is especially important.

If CPH revenue comes only from block rewards, the revenue stream depends primarily on token
issuance incentives and does not yet demonstrate new external cash flow across the system. When
external AI users buy and spend CPH to obtain real compute, the network gains genuine productive
demand.

Our goal is to create the following value loop:

AI companies contribute idle GPUs → earn CPH → our GPU network expands → external users pay
CPH for compute → CPH gains real utility demand → compute providers earn ongoing revenue → AI
companies reduce the net cost of infrastructure.

This is the most important strategic distinction between CPH and a simple mining coin.

5. From AI Tokens to a Compute Settlement Layer: The Higher-Level
Role of CPH
"AI is, in essence, mining" is a powerful analogy because both AI and PoW convert chips, electricity, and
time into digital output. When speaking to professional investors or technical partners, we make the
idea more precise.

In large language models, a token is a unit of text processing and billing; it is not a uniform unit of
physical compute. The compute consumption and cost of the same 1 million tokens can vary
substantially across models, context lengths, reasoning modes, precisions, and GPUs. Token prices from
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, or DeepSeek therefore cannot be converted directly on a simple one-to-
one basis. While the concept of exporting LLM tokens as a new form of “digital commodity” is highly
compelling, turning this vision into a scalable commercial reality still presents significant challenges.

AI tokens are consumable by nature—once used, they are gone. That leads us to a more precise
positioning for CPH:

A common settlement asset for heterogeneous AI compute transactions.

As a next step, we can establish a standardized Compute Unit that normalizes compute based on GPU
type, effective compute time, VRAM, memory bandwidth, workload type, latency, SLA, and other
relevant factors. The market can then quote a price in "CPH per Compute Unit."

This design allows the underlying hardware to use NVIDIA, AMD, Apple Silicon, or other AI chips and the
application layer to run entirely different models, while CPH provides a common mechanism for quoting,
payment, and incentives.

6. Four Direct Financial Benefits CPH Can Bring to AI Companies
1. Improve Lifetime GPU Utilization
We can turn idle hours that previously generated no revenue into hours that may generate revenue. For
CapEx that has already been committed, any period in which marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost
contributes additional capital recovery.

2. Extend the Economic Life of GPUs
Frontier AI competition may require rapid upgrades, while PoW and certain lower-priority compute
tasks do not require every generation of GPU to remain state of the art. Older GPUs can move from
"primary AI assets" to "secondary compute assets" and continue generating revenue instead of
immediately becoming sunk costs.

3. Establish a Potential Yield Floor for Hardware
If a specific GPU model can consistently generate positive marginal returns on the CPH network, its
value becomes less dependent on AI cloud rental rates alone. In theory, the present value of net mining
income can provide one reference point for hardware residual value, reducing uncertainty around
economic depreciation.

4. Turn AI Infrastructure into a Dual-Use Asset
We want the same GPU fleet to serve both AI and a decentralized network. As the CPH compute
marketplace comes online, the fleet can potentially earn both blockchain-security revenue and revenue
from real AI compute, making each GPU more like a "digital production machine" that can automatically
seek the highest-return opportunity across different markets.


7. What Future Awaits Us?
For us, this is the critical piece that will determine whether CPH can move beyond a grand vision and
become a genuine infrastructure solution that AI companies can incorporate into their balance sheets.

First, our go-to-market strategy begins with edge computing, validating CPH mining on devices such as
the AMD Max 395 to prove product compatibility, unit economics, and the operating model through
lower-cost, faster deployment cycles. Once validated, we will expand into high-performance enterprise
GPUs such as the H200 and B300, delivering greater compute density and economic efficiency for B2B

customers. This phased approach will enable CPH to scale from edge devices to data-center
infrastructure and establish a comprehensive, commercially scalable compute ecosystem.

Second, we need automatic scheduling. CPH workloads must remain low priority and preemptible.
When an AI job arrives, mining should exit quickly enough to preserve high-margin AI SLAs.

Third, we need to evolve the "mining market" into a "compute market." Block rewards can bootstrap
the network, but long-term value increasingly needs to come from real users purchasing training,
inference, and data-processing services. Buyer demand for CPH should be able to offset at least part of
the sell pressure created when compute providers receive tokens.

Fourth, we need a standardized Compute Unit and a transparent pricing system. Users can understand
prices in dollars, stablecoins, or fiat, while underlying settlement and node incentives use CPH. This
structure can align enterprise procurement practices with genuine CPH demand.

Fifth, we need to confront power constraints directly. If a data center is already power-constrained,
continuous mining could raise peak electricity costs or even displace higher-value AI workloads. Our
principle is clear: mine on the right equipment, at the right time, and at the right power price.


Conclusion: AI Creates the Highest Value; CPH Recovers the
Remaining Value
One of the defining contradictions of the AI infrastructure era is that humanity is buying chips at
unprecedented speed while making the previous generation lose its position of economic leadership at
an equally unprecedented pace.

That contradiction now carries a second layer of pressure: capital investment in upstream GPUs and
data centers continues to expand, while downstream per-token pricing power is continuously
pressured by open-source models and low-cost APIs. When the cost curve moves up and the unit-
revenue curve moves down, recovering GPU investment through model usage revenue alone becomes
increasingly difficult. AI companies therefore need a second and third infrastructure monetization
channel beyond token pricing.

Our goal is clear: even after a chip exits the most advanced AI workloads, we want it to retain a second
life that can continue generating revenue.

This is the position we want CPH to occupy.

AI creates the highest value from compute; CPH recovers the remaining value of compute.
